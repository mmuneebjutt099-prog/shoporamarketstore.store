How to Compare Energy Use and Lifetime Running Costs

Compare energy labels, power ratings, usage patterns and maintenance costs to estimate what an appliance or electronic product may cost over time.

Shopora Editorial Team · 8 min read · October 2026

Start with the product’s purpose and size

Compare products that perform the same job and are suitable for the same room, household or workload. A larger appliance may use more energy because it serves a larger need, while an undersized product may run longer or fail to meet expectations. Compare capacity, output and intended use before treating an energy figure as a direct ranking.

Read the energy label carefully

Where a recognised energy label is available, check its scale, test conditions and annual or typical-use estimate. Label systems vary by country and product category, so compare items assessed under the same system. A label is a useful guide, not a guarantee of the exact bill you will receive in your home.

Understand watts and kilowatt-hours

Wattage describes the rate at which a device uses power under specified conditions; energy bills usually charge for energy consumed over time. For a rough estimate, multiply power in kilowatts by hours of use to estimate kilowatt-hours. A 100-watt device running for five hours uses about 0.5 kWh if it draws that power continuously. Actual consumption can vary with settings and operating cycles.

Estimate your own usage pattern

Think about how often and how long you will use the product. A device used briefly each week may have little impact on the bill, while equipment that runs for many hours each day deserves closer comparison. For appliances that cycle on and off, the rated maximum power alone may overstate typical consumption. Use manufacturer estimates or measured data when available.

Use your local electricity rate

Multiply estimated kilowatt-hours by your applicable rate to approximate the energy charge. Your bill may include tiered rates, time-of-use prices, taxes or fixed charges, so a simple calculation is only an estimate. If you compare two products, use the same rate and usage assumptions for both. Do not treat a sample calculation as a promise of savings.

Look beyond energy consumption

Include purchase price, delivery, installation, filters, consumables, maintenance and likely repair costs. A more efficient product may have a higher initial price but lower running costs; whether it is worthwhile depends on the price difference and how long you expect to use it. Conversely, a premium efficiency rating may not recover its extra cost if the product is rarely used.

Check standby use and useful features

Some devices consume power while waiting in standby, connected to a network or charging. Look for standby information where relevant and check whether power-saving settings suit your routine. Avoid paying extra for features you will not use, but make sure any energy-saving mode does not interfere with the product’s main purpose.

Consider maintenance and replacement parts

Dirty filters, blocked vents, worn seals and poor maintenance can affect performance in some appliances. Read the recommended maintenance schedule and check whether replacement parts are available and affordable. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions; never modify electrical equipment or disable safety features to reduce energy use.

Make a fair comparison table

Record the product price, capacity, energy estimate, your expected hours of use, local rate, required consumables and warranty terms. Mark unknown values instead of inventing estimates. Compare the same time period—such as one year or the expected useful life—and explain any assumptions so the result remains meaningful.

Energy labels and electricity rates vary by country and provider. Use current local information and treat running-cost calculations as estimates.
